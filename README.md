**Foraging in turns: the located cost of remembering together**

**Abstract**
Much of what people remember is recalled in conversation, with two people taking turns to produce a single sequence. Recalling with others reduces what individuals retrieve, but it is not known at which point in an exchange this cost arises. Here, we used optimal foraging theory to track how one person's recall shapes another's at each turn, comparing each decision with the same person's decisions when recalling alone. One hundred and seventy participants studied words from several categories and recalled them individually, then in three written conversations with alternating turns, and then individually again. Treating a run of words from one category as a memory patch, we found that the cost of recalling together was concentrated at the moment a partner opened a new category, where patches were less than half as likely to continue as when recalling alone. This cost did not grow as the responder ran out of words in the category, but reflected competition between the partner's category and the responder's own. After the conversations, participants recalled more words, particularly partners' words whose category they had taken up, and their patches deepened mainly in the categories in which they had gained words, while differences between participants in how they organized their recall were preserved. These findings indicate that remembering together changes what people retrieve more than how they search memory, and suggest that shared memories are built from brief decisions made each time one person's recall meets another's.
